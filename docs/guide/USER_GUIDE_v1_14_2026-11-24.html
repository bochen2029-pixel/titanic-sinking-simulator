How this guide is made

A script builds this document from them, takes its pictures from the website build through the page's own controls, and pulls the tables of results from the repository's own test output, so the guide changes when the simulator does. Chapter 1

The simulator in one page

You stand the watch on the bridge of RMS Titanic at 11:39 pm on 14 April 1912. She makes 22½ knots on a course of 266°. Somewhere ahead is ice. What you order, and when, decides what the ice does to her. The flooding model then decides whether she sinks, and how. After the ice the night goes on: reports reach the bridge, the boats go down, the wireless calls for help. When she breaks, the page follows her two pieces down to the sea bed. Early access, a work in progress: the simulator is playable in its core loop, and it will change.

This edition

Since edition 1.13, the page says more plainly what is the model's and what is the record's: the Evidence tab's tables give the record in its own words, each witness's row its source in plain words, and its last section says where the model misses. In the desktop app a man who went away in a boat leaves the bridge when the model lowers her. A phone's first screen says where to begin, a room picked under her rooms comes into view with the credit for the deck plans, and a restart of the 1912 damage keeps her pumps and her list. Edition 1.14 describes the model as it stood on 24 November 2026 (the repository at 5f4bb4f). Before it, 1.13 made the simulator public, in early access, as version 0.3.0, at https://bochen.xyz/titanic/, with a plainer first minute; 1.12 let you speak to the bridge in the desktop app; 1.11 had her engineers raise the doors aft of boiler room 4 by hand and named the first-class passengers booked into her rooms; 1.10 added the bar back to any minute played; 1.9 ended a watch where she breaks; 1.8 let the men answer in the desktop app, in words a language model writes for them; 1.7 calibrated the model by a history match and added the app for Windows.

The simulator computes the sinking instead of animating it. A model of her hull and compartments works out, a quarter of a second at a time, where the water goes and how she floats with it. The 3D view only draws what the model says; the same model runs without it, in Node, for the tests (chapter 11).

What it models

  • Flooding. Sixteen watertight compartments, each split port and starboard and above and below E deck: 64 spaces, joined by 290 water paths. The paths are the holes in her shell, the watertight doors, the openings in the decks and the tops of the bulkheads.
  • How she floats. Her rise and fall, her trim and her list, as one rigid body, starting from her drafts on the night.
  • How she handles. Her turning and her stopping, fitted to the trial record of the three sister ships.
  • The iceberg. A body of ice she can miss, graze, dent or run her stem into. The contact makes the damage. No hole is painted on.
  • Her machinery. Coal, the fires in six boiler rooms, steam, two engines and a turbine, the steering engines, the dynamos, the watertight doors and the bilge pumps. Each fails when water reaches it, and what depends on it fails after it. The pumps take your orders.
  • Flooding at speed. While she moves, the sea presses harder at an opening that faces the flow, and her bow wave raises the water near the stem.
  • Her wake. On a watch, the waves her hull raises as it moves, and the white water where they break and in the races of her screws (chapter 3).
  • The rest of the night. Four readers of the watch follow it without changing it: the reports that reach the bridge, late and in words; the twenty boats, uncovered, filled and lowered; the wireless, the rockets and the other ships; and a gauge of the bending of her hull, which marks the moment her own loads reach what her hull could bear. Chapter 6 takes them in turn.
  • Her fall to the bed. After she breaks (on a damage preset, after she founders), her bow and her stern as two bodies of their own, carried 3,790 m down to the sea bed by their weight, the water and the air in them, and the sea about them. The wreck as surveyed is laid on the bed beside them.
  • How it looked and sounded. A Night look, the lookouts' eye in the crow's nest and the eye of someone in a boat off her beam; the ship heard, each sound following a number in the model (chapter 3); and her decks walked while she sinks, on the model's own water (chapter 6).

What it is fitted to

The flooding's uncertain inputs are calibrated by a history match against an observation ledger of the 1912 record: 30 sets of them that pass the record's tests are kept, and one of them (member C) is the model's defaults. They sample what the record does not rule out, and are not the whole of it. The fit is bounded so that it keeps the verdicts the record gives for Wilding's four and five compartments, Olympic after the Hawke collision and Britannic in 1916. Chapter 9 shows those cases, and sets the run beside Halpern's eyewitness reconstruction of her trim (2023). A few other constants are fitted in their own files, each named there: her handling, the encounter's area constant, the boats (3 numbers, to the launch record of 1912), the reports and the wake's scale.

In this build the 1912 damage gives: Founders at 2:25 am, 2 h 46 min after the damage. The reconstructed watch, on the night's own orders, breaks at 2:16 am, 156.5 minutes after she touched the ice. By the witnesses' accounts she broke between about 2:15 and 2:19 am, and was gone at 2:20, 160 minutes after the collision.

Read every outcome as an estimate

Several inputs are informed guesses with a physical meaning: the strength of her plating, the berg's mass, the force at which a bow crushes, how her engineers worked the pumps, how readily people came forward to the boats. The code labels each of them as a bet. A night's outcome is a well-founded estimate, not a verdict.

What it does not do

  • The break is a cut. On a watch the flooding model stops on the first step her own loads reach her strength somewhere along her, and the fall takes her from there, cut clean in two at that place. How her hull tore is not modelled: the engine has a joint that bends and tears before it lets go, but it is a what-if, and no default run uses it. A damage preset runs on to her foundering, where nothing gives way and her last minute is a plunge; only then is she cut in two, over boiler room 1, for her fall.
  • The fall is a first model. Each piece carries its air as one pocket. A watch hands her over where she breaks, about 7 degrees by the head and nearly upright, and on the 1912 orders both pieces land upright, as the wreck's do. A damage preset, and a watch with handover=flag in its address (a what-if), hand her over at the flag instead, far down by the head and on her beam ends: there her stern lands upright, but on the 1912 damage and orders her bow lands upside down, where the wreck's bow lies upright, and the card at her end says so. The bow lands farther from the break than the wreck's bow lies, and the air in her space by space is still to come (chapter 6).
  • No trapped air while she floats, but for one rule: the air shut in under a grand staircase's dome lifts its glazed cover once the doors of the stair hall are under water.
  • The decks above E deck are one space in each compartment, up through the superstructure, which keeps the sea out until its windows break. Water that comes in at a window is at once on the decks under it, where in the ship it had to find a stairwell first.
  • Speed is rough. Her speed reaches the holes only roughly: nobody measured the pressures on this hull. The damage presets lie stopped.
  • Only the 1912 damage and a watch are pumped. The other presets lie unpumped.
  • The list. The model's list to port falls short of the one witnesses saw late in the night, and its list to starboard just after the collision is smaller and shorter-lived than the 5° they saw (chapter 9).
  • People are counted, not drawn. The boats move places and minutes, and name nobody.
Chapter 2

Quick start: your first watch

The page is at https://bochen.xyz/titanic/. Ten minutes from opening it to your first forecast; a few more, at a faster clock, to her end.

  1. Open the watch. At the top of the Run tab, under Start here, press Take the watch: the bridge of 14 April 1912 is yours. Beside it, Watch the 1912 orders plays the night as it went, for you to take over with the wheel or the telegraph, and the chip the Evidence tab under them opens the page's own account of what the simulator is and what it is not. The first scenario card, The watch, 14 April 1912, opens the same watch, and so does a link that ends in #night=1912. The Bridge appears over the 3D view. Nothing is in sight yet, and the clock runs at real time.
  2. Keep the sound on if you want the lookout's bell, the telegraph's gong, the engines and the ship at sea. The box Sound on the Run tab is ticked from the start, and The ship at sea: volume beside it sets how loud the ship herself is. A browser plays sound only after your first tap or key; any will do.
  3. Wait for the bells. Three bells ring from the crow's nest, and the lookout calls "Iceberg right ahead!" The status turns to Ice ahead, and the Lookout readout on the Bridge gives the ice's range and bearing.
  4. Give your order. Put the wheel over with Hard to port or Hard to starboard, or the keys A and D. Ring the telegraph with its buttons, or step it with ↑ and ↓. The time from the bells to your first order is your reaction time.
  5. Watch her answer. She is slow to answer. The rudder goes over at 4 degrees a second, and it needs water moving past it to bite.
  6. Read the outcome. She clears the ice, or is dented, or has seams opened along her side, or runs her stem into it. The Run tab says which, in one sentence, and so does a notice on the stage. If she is holed and the clock still runs at her own pace, the notice gives her opening, then where to run it faster: under Time, on the Run tab.
  7. Read the forecast. Once the ice is astern, the model runs the watch on to the end in the background, and the line under the readouts gives the forecast. The table The same berg, another watch shows how the same drill goes with the order given sooner or later.
  8. Run the night on. Choose 60× or 900× under Time, at the top of the Run tab. The reports reach the bridge, the boats are uncovered and lowered, the wireless calls for help (chapter 6). When she breaks, or lies steady, or has passed the ice untouched, a card says what happened, and Copy a link to this watch gives an address that replays it. Once she has broken, the clock runs on while her two pieces fall to the bed: choose 10× before her end to watch them go.
  9. Try again. This night again repeats the same berg. Another night draws a new one. Watch the 1912 orders plays the night's own orders. Replay my watch plays yours back, exactly.
On the 1912 night

The order came about twenty seconds after the bells, and she was holed along her starboard side. In this build an order up to 10.5 seconds after the bells clears the berg; from 25.5 seconds she meets it stem first. Chapter 5 has the whole table.

Time runs at its own pace

On a watch the clock is held to real time until the ice is passed, so that your reaction time is real. After that, run it faster with the speed buttons under Time, at the top of the Run tab and above the watch's own section: 60× plays the rest of the night in under three minutes. When a report reaches the bridge, the clock drops to her own pace for a few seconds; the box When a report comes, the clock slows to her own pace for a few seconds turns that off. The bar under the speed buttons takes the night back to any minute already played, to play on from there (chapter 7).

The app for Windows

The same page comes as a portable app, from the latest release in the simulator's repository on GitHub: unzip Titanic-win-x64-<version>-<commit>.zip, then run Titanic.exe in the unzipped folder. It needs no browser and no installer, and keeps her settings in the data folder beside it. It connects to nothing, unless you switch on the crew's minds (to ask the bridge), Deepgram's voices (to hear the men) or speaking to the bridge (to give orders aloud) with a key of your own (chapter 4), and then only to the provider you chose, or to Deepgram. The app's own keys are F11 for full screen and F1 for About. About is headed with the version and opens with "Early access, a work in progress." It gives the 1912 watch's fingerprint and checks that this PC computes the same night, and it ends with the page's address on the web, where this guide is, and where to report a fault (the repository's Issues on GitHub). In the app, Copy a link to this watch copies the watch's address alone (#night=...&log=...): give it to the app as Titanic.exe "--open=#night=...&log=...". The zip's README.txt says what Windows may ask on the first run.

The night, and the eyes of people who were there

Night draws what a person there would have seen: a clear, moonless, flat-calm night, stars to the horizon and mirrored in the glassy sea, a black sea, her own lights. The berg is a dark mass a little paler than the sky, with no white water at its foot, which is why it was seen so late. Her lit ports shine in the water. A port stays lit while its section's circuit holds and her dynamos feed it. Under the sea it burns on, drawn dimmer, until its section's fuses blow or the dynamos stop (chapter 8). All of it is appearance, chosen in the code and marked there; X-ray is how the physics is read.

Her wake

On a watch the sea round her is the model's, not a drawing. Her hull raises a field of waves as it moves, worked out step by step from her speed, her turning and her drift; and white water is made where those waves are too steep to stand, in a sheet off each bow, and in the races of her three screws, as much as each shaft's thrust makes. The white water stays on the sea where it was made, spreads and dies away, so what she did is written on the water behind her. Ring Stop and the white road astern fades as her screws' races die; ring Full astern and the wing screws throw their races forward along her sides. Nothing of Kelvin's wedge is drawn by rule: the V of waves either side of her track, the waves across it and the lopsided wake of a turn come out of the field.

Wake, with the display switches, turns it off and on. It is on from the start, but on a phone, where it waits to be asked for. Off, the page draws what it drew before: a ribbon from her stern, a bow wave and Kelvin's lines. They stand in as well for a watch's first seconds, while the field is being made, and whenever the clock runs at 10× or faster, quicker than the field can follow. A damage preset lies stopped and has no wake.

Where the waves are and how high, and where white water is made, are the model's. How much white water each cause makes and how long it lasts are estimates, marked in the code (chapter 10). The drawing is appearance: the sheen and shade on the slopes, the lines of equal height every quarter of a metre in the X-ray look, the broken edges of the foam. In the Night look her own lights show the white water close about her; farther off it is a darker road on the black sea.

The ship heard

With Sound ticked, the ship has a sound of her own beside the bells, the telegraph's gong and the engines' beat. Each sound's loudness follows a number in the model; only the timbres are the page's own.

  • Her own wind and the sea along her side grow as the square of her speed. On a flat calm the only wind on her is her own, so when her engines stop the night goes quiet.
  • The engines rumble with the revolutions of the wing shafts.
  • The ice grinds with the force of each step of the contact, from the first blow to the end of the grind.
  • The water coming in rushes with the tons a minute coming in.
  • Her structure creaks as her trim changes.
  • The ship's bell strikes the half hours by her clock.

Each view hears its own mix: the wind and the bell on the Bridge and in the crow's nest, the engines and the water below the waterline, everything faint from the boat. The ship at sea: volume sets the level. The sound is silent when switched off or paused, or once she has broken or foundered, and it fades as the clock runs faster than her own pace. Nobody has tuned the levels by ear yet. The men's voices on the bridge are not part of it: they have a switch of their own (chapter 4).

On a phone

Chapter 4

The Bridge: wheel and telegraph

Two controls ran the ship in 1912: the wheel, through the steering engines, and the engine-order telegraph to the engine room. The Bridge gives you both, with the instruments to read her answer, and on a watch you can give the bridge's orders in words, typed or, in the desktop app, spoken, and hear the men answer aloud.

The wheel, and the 1912 orders

In 1912 helm orders were tiller orders, the reverse of today's. "Hard-a-starboard" put the tiller to starboard, the rudder to port, and swung her head to port. Each button says both:

The slider between the buttons sets any angle up to 40 degrees either side. The steering engines move the rudder at 4 degrees a second (a guess: it was never measured), so hard over from midships takes 10 seconds. They need steam: without it the rudder stays where it is.

What the rudder needs

The rudder works only on water moving past it. Stopped, she will not turn however hard you put it over. Going astern, the rudder works the other way, and weakly. The faster she goes, the faster she turns, but the further she runs while turning. With her screws going astern, their wash creeps forward along her sides.

Orders to the bridge, in words

On a watch the Run tab has a section Orders to the bridge, under the watch's own. It opens by saying what it is: you are the captain, the officer of the watch answers below and carries your order out, and the order line needs no key and sends nothing anywhere. Type an order as you would say it in the line Give an order and press Enter, or Give. The words become the same order the wheel, the telegraph or a button gives: it is logged at its step, and a link to the watch replays it.

Under the line, Try offers three orders: Hard a-starboard, Stop and Close the watertight doors. A chip only writes its words into the line, and nothing is given until you press Enter or Give. The chips go once she has gone, with no one left on the bridge to take an order.

  • Say it as the bridge would: "hard a-starboard", "port ten", "midships", "full astern", "port engine stop", "close the watertight doors", "uncover the boats", "women and children first", "send the call for assistance", "fire a rocket", "sound the ship", "go forward and look".
  • Several orders in one line are given in the order said: "hard a-starboard and full astern".
  • A line that is not understood whole gives no order at all, and the bridge says what it did not understand.
  • The bridge's talk, under the line, keeps the last lines said: yours, the answer of the man who took the order, and the orders the night's own script gives. The newest line shows itself for a moment as it comes. Until anything has been said, the talk reads "Nothing has been said yet." and says that your orders and the officers' answers come there.

The switch Helm orders has two positions, 1912 helm orders (the default) and Modern. In 1912 a helm order named the side the tiller went to, as the wheel's buttons show: "hard a-starboard" turns her head to port. The modern way names the side the rudder goes to. The line under the switch says which is in force, and the quartermaster's answer says which way her head goes. An order that names the rudder, the wheel or where her head should go ("turn to port") gives no order: the quartermaster answers with the helm order that would do it.

The line is the page's own: it needs no language model, no key and no network. A typed order takes the watch over, as the wheel does: after it the night's own orders are no longer followed, and the talk says so. In the desktop app you can also say an order aloud (below).

Ask the bridge: the crew's minds (the desktop app)

In the desktop app you can speak to the men on her bridge in your own words, and each answers in character: the officer of the watch Mr Murdoch, Mr Moody, Mr Boxhall, Quartermaster Hichens, and Mr Andrews while he is on the bridge. The words are a language model's, and every such line is marked imagined. Each man knows only what has reached the bridge, by a report, an order or his own eyes: never the water you can see in X-ray, and nothing of how the night ends.

Chapter 5

Playing a watch

The bells and your reaction time

The lookout rings three bells when the ice comes within the night's sighting range. On the 1912 night that is about 500 yards; in this build the bells ring with the ice 494 m off. Your reaction time runs from the bells to your first order to the wheel or the engines. An order given before the bells is logged, but it is not a reaction: nobody had seen anything yet, so the panel calls it hindsight. Orders to the doors, the stokehold, the pumps, the boats and the wireless do not count.

The four outcomes

When the encounter is over (the ice is abaft the beam and out of reach, or she has stopped, or she has lain against it for ten seconds), the Run tab and a notice give the outcome in one sentence:

Once she is past the ice the verdict is kept: after a miss, the line under the readouts still gives it while she circles past the berg again, and on a laptop's screen the card says "Close this card to take the wheel and the telegraph again".

On the 1912 orders the sentence is Holed: 0.98 m2 of seams open along the starboard side, the forepeak to boiler room 5. Night 16 with no order ends Stem into the ice: bow crushed in 20 m, 0.12 m2 of seams open along the port side. Open to the sea: the forepeak to No. 1 hold.

When she is holed and the clock still runs at her own pace, 1×, the notice gives her opening first, then where to run the clock faster: "Run the clock faster under Time, on the Run tab, to see what the water does". The compartments holed are in the line under the readouts and on the Run tab.

After the ice: the forecast

Once the encounter is over, the clock is free: run it faster with the speed buttons under Time, at the top of the Run tab. The page then runs a copy of the whole watch on to the end, in the background: her plant, the engineers' rules, every order given and the scripted orders still to come all go with it, and the line under the readouts gives the forecast. Its time is the watch's own, and it runs to where she breaks: on the 1912 orders it reads Breaks at 2:16 am, 2 h 36 min after the touch, and she breaks 156.5 minutes after the touch in the forecast and 156.5 when the watch is run to her end. Where she would founder before her own loads reach her strength, it says when she founders instead. An order to the ship given after the ice (to the pumps, say) brings a new forecast. An order to the boats, the reports or the wireless changes nothing in the model and brings none, unless it ends the 1912 orders before their Stop. If she will float, the forecast says so, with her final trim and the water aboard.

The model is one calibration, and the record allows others. The calibration's history match kept 30 sets of its numbers that pass the record's tests: the kept set. They sample what the record does not rule out, and are not the whole of it. Each was run through the 1912 night, and the paragraph under Forecast on the Run tab says how they end. On the 1912 orders, 30 of 30 break, between 2:07 am and 2:24 am, 2:17 am in the middle. The landed member, the model's own defaults and this watch's, breaks at 2:16 am. The kept set ran two runs only: this watch on its recorded orders at 22½ knots, and the 1912 damage as built. So once you give an order of your own, or on any other night, the page shows your run alone. A result is told as k of N, never as one model's word.

When a watch ends

When she breaks, lies steady with water aboard, or has passed the ice untouched, a card on the stage says what happened, in plain words, the model's figures read at that moment: when she broke and how long after the touch; what the ice did; when the first order came; what her pumps put back over the side; and the watch's fingerprint. On the night of 1912 the line after the time she broke says that this is the model's time, and sets the record's beside it: when, by the witnesses' accounts, she broke, and when she was gone. Where she founders instead, as on a watch handed over at the flag (a what-if: handover=flag in the address), the card says when she foundered, beside the record's own time. Her steam and her lights get lines of their own, each at the plant's own time and in the order they came:

  • her boilers and steam mains giving way as she rose;
  • where her main dynamos stopped before her emergency sets, the time only her emergency lights burned after (on the 1912 night both stop on one step);
  • her lights going out; or, where they never did, still burning when she broke, as on the 1912 orders, or burning to her end where she founders.

Nights, seeds and levels

  • A night is its seed and its orders. Every order is logged with the step of the model it was given at, so Replay my watch plays a watch again exactly. Any order makes the watch yours and lets it be replayed, as the wheel does: on the 1912 orders, those still to come are dropped. A hole made with the damage brush is the one exception: it is logged and replayed like any order, but the night's own orders go on (chapter 7). The watch's fingerprint is made from her state at every step. The Run tab stamps it once, on the step the encounter ends, at the end of its sentence about the watch ("Watch …, step …"), and the card gives it again at her end. The same seed and orders give the same fingerprint on the same browser. Across engines the model computes its own functions, so the page reaches the end of the 1912 encounter with Node's own fingerprint; past it, where the power function is still the engine's, two engines may part in the last bit (chapter 10).
  • Another night draws a berg from a seed: its size, its depth, where it lies off her track, and how far off the lookout sees it. To choose one, type a seed in Night (seed) and press Go or Enter. Seed 1912 is the night of 1912.
  • The ice on a drawn night sets how hard the next drawn night is: Seen early, Any night (the default), or Like 1912, seen late and close to her track. It never changes the 1912 watch.
  • Her speed, knots sets the speed of the next watch: 22½, 18, 15 or 10.
  • The address carries the night, for example #night=1912, with &kn=18 or &level=early when they are not the defaults, and &log= with the orders when it came from the card.

After the ice

The encounter takes a minute or two; what follows took two and a half hours, and most of it could only be watched. Four readers of the watch follow the rest of the night. Each reads the model and keeps an account of its own, and none changes the night: a watch has the same fingerprint with all of them as with none.

Walking her decks

When she breaks (on a damage preset, when she founders) the walk ends.

What you meet is the model's: her side where her hull is; her watertight bulkheads on every deck below their tops, with their doors open to you only while the model has them open; and the water of each of her 64 spaces at its own level, the levels the X-ray hull draws, with the bunker's and the peak tank's water where they stand. Where she would break

The studies' range is a figure for one place. The model also works out what her hull could bear at every station along her, in bending and in shear, from her plates and the riveted joints between them, and holds the gauge's bending and shear against it as she settles. The first time the two meet anywhere along her, a red line appears under the gauge: She would break here, now, then the station in metres forward (+) or aft (−) of amidships, the space it lies in, and the ship's time. In the X-ray hull a red ring marks the place. Hold the pointer over the line for the figures: the bending moment against what the hull could bear there, the shear against what it could carry, her trim and her list.

On a watch she breaks there. The watch ends on that step: the status reads Breaking, the card on the stage She broke, and her fall begins from there (below). The forecast after the ice runs to the same step, so it gives the time she breaks. In this build the onset comes over boiler room 2, about 5 metres forward of boiler room 1, where the wreck parted: at 2:16:28 am on the 1912 orders and at 2:19:24 am on the 1912 damage. On the night itself, by the witnesses' accounts, she broke between about 2:15 and 2:19 am, and was gone at 2:20. But the onset comes at about 7 degrees of trim, flatter than every published analysis of the break (five put it at 10 to 17 degrees, one at 23, the older picture at 45 or more; no witness gave an angle), and six to seven minutes before she founders on the 1912 damage, or on a watch carried on to its flag, where the record's own times leave from under a minute to about five between her break and her going. Nothing in her strength or her weight was tuned to put it there. On a damage preset nothing breaks there: the line marks the moment, the model carries on to her flag, and her last minute is a plunge.

Her fall to the bed

A watch lets her pieces go where she breaks: on the first step her own loads reach her strength, about 7 degrees by the head and nearly upright, cut clean in two at that place. Her systems

Her machinery depends on itself, so a failure spreads. The Systems tab shows each link and its state now: green when it works, amber when it is short, red when it has failed, grey when it is idle.

Her lights and the doors

Her lights are the dynamos' output, not a clock. Water at the dynamo flat, or too little steam, puts the main sets out. Two small emergency dynamos on D deck then keep about 15 % of her light (a guess), until the sea reaches D deck where they stand. When the main dynamos stop, so does the current that drops the watertight doors from the bridge: in the dark the bridge's switch does nothing, and only each door's float, or its lever worked by hand, will drop it.

The sea puts her lights out piece by piece before that. As a greaser agreed at the British inquiry, flooding blows the fuses. The model divides her lighting into 250 sections, each a deck's space in one compartment, on one side, with a main circuit and an emergency circuit. How her mains were in fact divided is not known, so the sections are a guess. When the sea inside a section reaches its switches, its fuses go, for good, whatever the dynamos are doing. The model takes the switches as 1.2 m over the floor, a guess too. Her lights' node in the graph below turns amber with the first section dark, while the dynamos still run, and says how many are dark: for example "40 of 250 sections dark: the sea in them has blown their fuses". Her ports follow the same circuits. A port the sea has covered burns on, drawn dimmer, until its section's fuses blow or her dynamos stop.

On the 1912 watch, after she touches the ice, her engineers raise doors by hand, as a trimmer and a greaser who worked them told the British inquiry. Thirty minutes after the touch they raise the doors in bulkheads G, H, J and K, to reach the pumps and valves, on the chief engineer's order. Sixty-two minutes after it they raise those in L, M, N and O, to fetch the 10-inch suction pipe. On the 1912 orders that is 12:10 am and 12:42 am. A door is raised only while the rooms on both sides of it are dry, and the bridge's switch is put off for it, as raising a door by hand needed. Its float stays armed, so the sea drops it again as it comes. This is the engineers' own work, not an order of the bridge's: it goes in no log, and no report of it reaches the bridge, which had nothing to show which doors stood open. On the Systems tab the Doors link names the doors they raised, with the ship's time, why, and its source in the record; on the Bulkheads tab, pointing at such a door's Open says the same. On the 1912 orders the night is the same as without them, bit for bit, up to where she breaks, and there the doors in J to O still stand open into dry rooms. The damage presets and the drawn nights carry no such schedule.

Orders to the pumps

The section Pumps on the Systems tab says who has the pumps, and lists each room with water in it or pumps on it: its water, what its pumps put out, and each pump on it, at work or at work in so many minutes. On a watch it lists every other room an order can draw from as well, dry and in a quieter form, so that pumps can be put on a room before the water reaches it; and it takes your orders:

  • Its own pumps puts a room's own pumps on it, at work 5 minutes after the order.
  • Every pump that can reach it adds every free pump that can, those standing elsewhere led to it through the bilge main, at work 30 minutes after the order.
  • One pump… opens a list of the free pumps, each with its rate and when it would be at work.
  • Off beside a pump, or All off for a room, takes pumps off at once.
  • Back to the engineers hands the pumps back to their rules, which go on from where the pumps stand.

An order takes the pumps from the engineers, who then wait for yours. A room's suctions and the two mains limit what can be drawn from it; none works without steam, and a pump stops when the room it stands in floods. An order that cannot be carried out changes nothing and says why. Every order goes in the watch's log, and the forecast runs the watch on with it.

Blowing off

When the engines stop with every fire lit, the safety valves lift and steam roars from the escape pipes on her funnels, until the stokers bank the fires. On the 1912 orders it starts soon after the telegraph rings Stop. With the sound on, you hear it; in the 3D view, the steam rises from her funnels.

Chapter 10

How the model works, briefly

Each piece below is a model with numbers in it. Where a number comes from a source the code names the source; where it had to be guessed the code says so. This chapter says which is which.

Flooding

Her hull is a shape fitted to the builder's hydrostatics, cut into 4,408 vertical columns. Every quarter of a second the model adds up her buoyancy and the water in her over those columns, at the pose she has at that moment, and moves her as one rigid body in rise, trim and list. Water moves between the 64 spaces and the sea through orifice and weir laws: through holes, doors and deck openings, and over the tops of the bulkheads. The two halves of an open space share one water surface.

Above B deck stand the three tiers of her superstructure. They belong to the spaces above E deck, so they hold her up while they are dry. Their windows, and the entrance doors and domes of the two grand staircases, keep the sea out until the water on them reaches their strength: about a metre at the sill of a wooden door, a metre and a half on a window, half a metre on a dome's glazed cover. A dome goes sooner than that, from below: once the doors of its stair hall are under water, the air shut in under it lifts the cover's panes. On the 1912 damage the first dome gives way at 2:23 am, minutes before she founders at 2:26 am. No survivor saw a dome give way; it is a reconstruction, in the model as in the paintings and the film.

The flooding model's uncertain inputs are calibrated by a history match against an observation ledger of the 1912 record (chapter 9): 30 sets of them that pass the record's tests are kept, and one of them (member C) is the model's defaults. The fit is bounded so that it keeps the verdicts the record gives for Wilding's four and five compartments, Olympic after the Hawke collision and Britannic in 1916, and it held the reconstructed watch, run on to her foundering, to 150 to 170 minutes after the touch. The inputs it samples include the total area of the iceberg's damage (0.98 m² in member C, about 10.5 square feet, shared out over six compartments as Hackett and Bedford give its openings: 4.8, 11.9, 24.6, 26.2, 22.2 and 10.3 % from the forepeak aft, in the table of Felkins, Leighly and Jankovic, 1998), two of the water's paths (down through E deck, and through the deck above once it is under water), the bunker's opening and the head it bears, her stability and her list before the collision. Its runs of the 1912 night include the pumps as they were worked, and the superstructure with its windows and staircases. Along E deck, over the bulkheads that stop there, the water runs aft by two passages at their widths on the deck plans, which a number fitted before the history match, and held by it, multiplies: Scotland Road to port, 2.24 m between its walls, and the passage to starboard, 1.40 to 1.49 m.

Two spaces hold smaller ones. With her coal list to port, the empty starboard half of boiler room 5's forward bunker is a pocket of its own: the openings the ice made inside it fill it, its door leaks into the stokehold, and it gives way once the water in it stands high enough, emptying into boiler room 5, on the 1912 damage about an hour after the collision. Until then the stokehold's plates stay dry, as the leading stoker Barrett, who was there, told the inquiry. At her bow, the forepeak's lowest part, from the keel to the orlop deck's watertight flat, is her peak tank, a pocket that never gives way: the ice's openings there fill it, and the sea rises into the forepeak above only by the tank's vent, a 4-inch pipe led to G deck (its size is a bet). So the forepeak stays dry for its first minutes, as the British inquiry's report has it and as the lamp trimmer Hemming found its stores soon after the jar; on the 1912 watch the tank is full some 22 minutes after the collision, and the water comes up its vent from then on.

Her rooms are not yet part of the flooding. The model can cut each of its 64 spaces at her decks into 250 slabs, one deck's space in one compartment on one side, and share a space's water out among them by its own rule. Her rooms themselves are read from Bruce Beveridge's deck plans, as Encyclopedia Titanica publishes them free, into tables of facts, 572 rows on all nine decks from the orlop to the Boat deck, each with its source and its check against a 1912 original where one could be reached (The Shipbuilder's plates). The flooding does not use them yet, nor does the walk draw them: inside a space her decks are open floor. The page reads them to find a room and say when the water came to it, and the Cave list of 1912 puts names on the first-class staterooms it booked (chapter 7). What the tables find against the model, such as two of her bulkheads standing a little aft of the model's and her hull too narrow at her ends above E deck, is the next work for her rooms.

Handling

A standard manoeuvring model (the MMG model) with her hull, three screws and one rudder. Four numbers are fitted to the trial record the three sister ships share: Olympic's two points of turn in about 37 seconds, Titanic's turning circle of 3,850 feet, circles that barely change with speed, and the crash stop of about 3,000 feet, run as the trial was, with the engines reversed more slowly than usual. Her resistance follows Holtrop's method, and her screws going astern follow the Wageningen B-series' measurements. The screws' places were measured on a side plan of 1911.

The iceberg

The berg is a free body of ice. Where her side presses into it, the plating dents against the ice's crushing pressure, and the riveted seams open once a dent passes a set depth. The opened area is fitted so that the 1912 watch makes the same opening as the calibrated damage, shared out over her compartments the same way: one fitted factor for each, since the contact opens every torn metre alike. Head on, her bow crushes at a force that rises with the depth already crushed, from crushing laws for ship sections. The berg's face stays rigid in a stem strike.

These are the least certain numbers in the simulator: the plating's strength, the berg's mass, the seam gap for each metre of dent and the crushing force are all guesses with a physical meaning. And the 1912 point grazes the edge of a stem strike: a metre either way changes the damage a good deal.

Where the tear ends aft is fitted to the record. On the 1912 orders it ends inside boiler room 5's starboard forward bunker, about two feet abaft the bulkhead, where the British inquiry and the leading stoker Barrett put that room's damage, and the stokehold's own side stays whole. The rule covers that room's side only and rests on the 1912 record: on any other night it is a bet. Farther aft, on every night tried, the ice only dents her plating (docs/research/RESEARCH_br5_damage_extent_G6_v1_2026-11-20.md).

Flooding at speed

While she moves, the sea's level at each hole is raised by the share of her speed that strikes it head on, and near the stem by her bow wave. Stopped, it adds nothing. The 1912 damage is mostly slits along the flow, so for the night as it happened speed changes little: about a third of a minute. A bow crushed head on faces the flow, and takes the whole of it. The shares are guesses: nobody measured the pressures on this hull.

Her wake

Her wake is the sea's answer to her hull. The hull is a sheet of sources on her centre plane (Michell's thin ship), driven by her speed, her drift and her turning; the sea answers as linear waves on deep water, each wave travelling at its own exact speed, in two squares of sea that follow her, about 3 km across for the long waves and 1 km for the short. Thin-ship theory makes her waves too high, so their heights are scaled until the energy they carry away is what Holtrop's method, the one her resistance follows, spends on making waves. The white water is laid where that sea is too steep to stand, off her bows and in her screws' races, and left where it was made. How much each cause makes and how long it lasts are guesses. The wake reads her motion and feeds nothing back: a watch has the same fingerprint with it as without.

The method has been run unchanged on three hulls whose waves were measured in tanks. On the KRISO container ship its waves fall where they were measured, along the hull and in two cuts out to 35 m from its side, at about two-thirds of their height, and farther out they arrive early. On two hulls without a bulb, the Series 60 and Wigley's, the waves along the hull are again where they were measured; the Series 60's troughs amidships come out shallower than its tank's, about 0.6 as deep at her speed. Her bow wave against her plating follows a law: the height Noblesse and others (2008) give for a stem of her half-angle, 21°, measured on Bob Read's plans of Olympic. It replaced a fixed factor that no one value could make right (the Series 60 wanted 3.69, Wigley's hull 2.76, her stem about 7), and at full speed it raises her crest from 3.1 m to 5.7 m. The white water keeps a factor of its own. The notes are docs/research/RESULT_k6_kcs_benchmark_v1_1_2026-11-20.md and the Series 60 and Wigley results beside it.

The pumps and the doors

The pumps run on steam, at rates from the British inquiry's report, from the bottoms of the rooms with suctions. When the engineers started them on the night, and which they led where, is a guess for each room. The watertight doors fall shut over 25 seconds once released, by the bridge's switch, by their floats or by hand. On the 1912 watch the engineers raise the doors aft of boiler room 4 by hand, at about the times and for the reasons the British inquiry heard (chapter 8).

The readers of the night

The boats, the reports, help and the hull gauge are readers: modules that read the watch after each step of the model and keep an account of their own beside it. None writes into the flooding, the motion, the ice or the plant, so a watch has the same fingerprint with every reader and with none, and the repository's suites hold each of them to that.

  • The boats move places and minutes, fitted to the launch record of 1912 with 3 numbers: how readily people came forward below and above a trim of about 3.5 degrees, and the minutes a party gives a boat before it lowers her. The hands' rates are the record's; the rest are named guesses.
  • The reports follow rules: who could see a fact of the model, from where, and how long his news took to reach the bridge. One constant is fitted, to the builder's man's estimate of an hour to an hour and a half.
  • Help places the other ships where the research found them, with their speeds, their operators' hours and the time each took to get under way. The wireless is a load on her plant: it needs the dynamos, or the battery.
  • The hull gauge weighs her along her length, layer by layer, to the model's own mass and centre, and takes the bending moment of her hull at the pose and the water of the moment. Her hull's own steel is laid out as Prohaska's trapezoid, its ends at 0.66 of her mean weight per metre and its middle third at 1.17. That is its one fitted number, fitted to the Marine Forensics Panel's bending moment of 4.64 GN m with 35,000 t of water aboard. It was refitted once: when her ends above E deck were restored, and her water came to be weighed as the model's own loads weigh it. The 0.40 before had stood in for the buoyancy her missing ends would have given. Her fall weighs its two pieces by the same curve. The range it is read against is the studies'. Beside it the onset holds the gauge's bending and shear at every station against her strength there (below), and marks the first step at which, somewhere along her, the two together reach it. On a watch that step is her end: the night hands her to her fall there. The mark itself writes nothing into the model.

Her strength along her length

What her hull could bear is worked out from her cross-section at each station: the keel and the bottom, the side plating, the double bottom, and B and C decks, which carried her strength, each plate with its thickness and each riveted joint with its strength, from the sources the code names (Mengot, Walker in 1912, Halpern, Wilding, the Marine Forensics Panel, among others), with the funnels' uptakes cut out of the decks. Smith's method of progressive collapse then bends the section further and further, adding up what each plate still carries, until the whole gives way. Amidships her hull could bear about 5.3 GN m with her ends bending down, inside the studies' range, and its bottom gives way first. One figure no source gives, C deck's plating against B deck's, is fitted to the panel's section; nothing was fitted to her strength itself.

Her fall to the bed

On a watch the fall takes over from the flooding model at the onset, where she breaks, and her hull is cut clean in two at the onset's station (how it tore is not modelled). On a damage preset, and on a watch handed over at the flag (a what-if), it takes over at the flag, and her hull is cut over boiler room 1 (a rule for now). Each piece is a rigid body built from the model's own slices: its steel, the water in it (the pockets' water with it), and the air still in it, one pocket that the sea squeezes as it goes down. Since edition 1.12 the fall weighs the water in her peak tank, some 330 t at her stem, as the flooding model and the hull gauge weigh it, where before it took the tank's room as solid and left its water out; and each slice's cross-section is the model's whole column profile, where before it was thinned to about twelve points a side. Handed over where she breaks, nearly upright, the pocket's lift acts where her air lay then, kept fixed in her as she turns; handed over at the flag, on her beam ends, it acts, slice by slice, through the middle of the part the sea can fill. Each moves in all six ways a body can, its attitude free to pass through any angle. The water acts on it slice by slice: the mass of water it must move with it, the drag of the flow across it and along it, a lift where the flow leaves a torn end, and buoyancy at the density of an April column of sea, which grows with depth. On the bed the mud bears it up until it lies still. The coefficients come from experiments and textbooks, each named in the code; the one number the trials fit, where her weight acts along her, is not used on the page, and the time to the bed is checked against the reconstructions, not fitted to them. So the page shows what the model computes: on the 1912 orders her bow comes to rest about 1.6 km from the point under the break, some 0.4 km farther than in edition 1.11, and her stern about 50 m from it, where the wreck's bow lies about 0.8 km and its stern about 150 m from it.

The engine can also keep the two pieces joined at the break, on a hinge that bends, tears and lets go when the force across it passes what is left to hold it. That joint is a what-if: no default run uses it, and the page cuts her clean. A watch hands her to the fall at the onset by default: the night carries the onset's check itself, so the watch's forecast and every copy of it meet the same onset. Handing a watch over at the flag, as before edition 1.9, is the what-if (handover=flag in the page's address).

Her as numbers: Blender and Unreal

The model can hand her to another program.

  • node tools/export_ship.js writes the ship as numbers in one file. It is built from the model's own tables and functions and from the drawn ship's, with nothing placed by hand and every source named.
  • node tools/record_night.js 1912 auto writes a watch, every step of it. Every reader of the watch is attached, as the page attaches them, and --fall carries it on to the bed.

Both files have a fixed form: two runs give the same bytes. A second viewer then draws what the model computed. Its look (colours, light) is its own, and marked as such. Blender scripts in the repository do this:

  • one builds her hull and topsides from the export and checks them against it (U1);
  • another builds the model's 64 spaces as named volumes, each against its capacity;
  • a third builds, from a recorded night at three moments after the touch: the water inside her, with the pockets' own (the bunker of the fire and the forepeak tank); her lights, each going out when the sea inside its section blows its fuses; her steam; and her smoke. It then reads them back (U3).

They need Blender 5.2, so they are not in the gate. The same two files are meant for Unreal.

Both were rendered on 2026-11-23 in Blender 5.2.3 LTS (EEVEE), from the recorded 1912 night of the calibrated model (member C, fingerprint 48b71712), by the repository's U3 tools at commit 2a9f79e, with her hull and the drawn ship carrying her ends, and committed in 4928220. They are not retaken with this edition's pictures. What they show is the record's; the views, the colours and the light are appearance.

Same night, same result

The model steps a quarter of a second at a time and adds its sums in a fixed order, so a night replays bit for bit on the same JavaScript engine. It computes its own sine and cosine, exponential and logarithms, tangent and arc tangents, hyperbolic tangent, arc sine and arc cosine, cube root and the length of a vector: fdlibm's routines (V8's for the length) in plain arithmetic, the same in every engine that keeps the IEEE 754 standard and equal to Node's on every value tried. So a browser reckons the night as Node does: in the repository's test the page in Edge reaches the end of the 1912 encounter with Node's own fingerprint, and her fall to the bed takes the model's own functions as well. The power function is the exception, which the model still takes from the engine because changing it moves the calibration. Where two engines part, they part in the last digit, and the night can carry it: a change in the last bit moves the 1912 damage's foundering by up to 3.3 s (the validation table prints that floor). The forecast after the ice is a copy of the watch run on, so it gives the watch's own end. Her fall to the bed is reckoned the same way: the same hand-over gives the same fall, bit for bit.

Where it is least certain

The ice: its crushing pressure over a large area of contact, its mass, the plating's strength, and the 1912 point itself. Then the list late in the night, which the model does not reproduce. Then the engineers' practice with the pumps, and how readily people came forward to the boats. Then her fall: the air in her pieces, and the moment and the pose in which they are let go. Every guess is marked [bet] in the code, with its reasoning in the research notes under docs/research/.

Glossary

WordMeaning
TrimHer tilt fore and aft. By the head is bow down, and counts as positive.
ListHer tilt to one side, port or starboard.
BulkheadA watertight wall across the hull. Fifteen, lettered A at the bow to P at the stern, without I.
CompartmentThe space between two bulkheads: the forepeak FP, the holds H1 to H3, boiler rooms BR6 to BR1, the engine rooms RE, TE and EL, the shaft tunnels T1 and T2, the after peak AP.
DecksLettered downward from A: B, C, D and E deck are named in this guide. A bulkhead's top reaches B, D or E.
WayHer motion through the water. A rudder needs it.
TelegraphThe bridge's dial for engine orders, rung to the engine room.
Wing screwsThe two outer propellers, turned by the reciprocating engines. The centre screw is the turbine's.
SeamsThe joints between her plates, riveted. Opened by the ice, they let the sea in along her side.
StemThe foremost edge of her bow.
SeedThe number a drawn night is made from. The same seed gives the same night.
FingerprintEight characters made from her state at every step of a watch: equal fingerprints, equal watches.
BetThe code's word for a number that had to be guessed, with its reasoning beside it.
ReaderA part of the model that follows a watch and keeps an account of its own (the boats, the reports, help, the hull gauge and its onset) without changing the night.
CommutatorThe bridge's instrument for her list: a pendulum against a scale.
Davits, fallsThe arms that swing a boat out over the side, and the ropes she is lowered by.
CQD, SOSThe distress calls of the wireless: the old one, and the new one she sent beside it.
Bending momentHow hard her hull is being bent, here in GN m (a billion newton metres): the weight of her flooded bow and her rising stern against the buoyancy along her length.
Hogging, saggingHer hull bent with its middle up and its ends down, as when her bow and her stern hang on her middle; or the other way.
OnsetThe first moment her bending and shear somewhere along her reach what her hull could bear there: where and when she would break. On a watch she breaks there: the watch ends and her fall begins. On a damage preset it is a mark, and nothing breaks.
Kelvin's wedgeThe V of waves a ship leaves on deep water, about 19½° either side of her track.
SurveyThe wreck as found and mapped on the bed, 3,790 m down: the bow, the stern, and the field of boilers and wreckage where she broke.
Point under the breakWhere she broke, on the sea above the bed, as her fall takes it: the cut between her two pieces when her fall began (on a damage preset, when she foundered).
